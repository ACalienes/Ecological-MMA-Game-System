    Closing the Distance (WIP)

    Open Space · WrestlingClosing the Distance

    Provisional (WIP): derived from class recording 2026-08-21

    Run live as a three-layer progression. Pending review on the platform. Name and structure may change. Roles are named Closer and Spacer rather than offense and defense, deliberately. See the note under The Matchup.

    Open SpaceWrestlingCombinedIntermediateAccess → Transition

    The man who wants to grab you has almost nothing. The man who wants to stay free has almost everything, including the takedown. Close that gap anyway.

    StartTwo fighters in open space at honest striking range, inside a marked perimeter.
    →
    The GoalThe Closer wants a clinch above the hips that the Spacer cannot leave. The Spacer wants distance, or the legs.
    →
    FinishClinch held through an escape attempt → Closer · Clean separation, redirect, or connection on the legs → Spacer · Leave the perimeter → loss.

    There is no clock on the entry.
    Collect enough to be sure, then go once.

    Reaching is how you get hit. You do not have to arrive quickly, you have to arrive. Every step forward is priced, so buy the one that works.

    What to Read

    Attune to the retraction of the jab, not its launch: the window opens as the arm comes home, and a lazy return opens it wider. Read weight distribution through the lead foot, because a Spacer who has just committed a cross has weight in front of their hips and cannot step back cleanly for a beat. Read hip-to-hip distance rather than glove distance, since gloves lie about how far away someone really is. For the Spacer, read the quality of the forward momentum: honest pressure can be met, but clumsy, over-committed pressure is the thing you steer past, and the difference is visible in whether their head has travelled ahead of their feet.

    The Starting Position

    PlayersTwo, one Closer, one Spacer.
    RangeHonest MMA striking range. Not the false close range that appears when nobody can punch.
    BoundaryA marked perimeter, both stay inside.
    RolesCloser: no strikes at rung 1, a jab from rung 2. Spacer: a jab throughout, plus one resetting cross from rung 3, and the level change to the legs.
    Start & reset"Go". The winner switches roles. Rotate groups with the winner staying in.

    The Matchup

    🤝
    Closer (Forward pressure)

    Trying to arrive at a clinch above the hips that holds when the other man tries to leave.

    You do not want to box and you do not want to wrestle from out here. You want to be sticky. Use the jab to open a door, not to score. Control with the non-punching hand while the punching hand works. Do not reach: reaching is exactly when you get hit.
    VS
    📏
    Spacer (Keeping distance)

    Trying to stay free, punish the entry, or drop under the hips and connect on the legs.

    Build a wall of jabs so walking in costs something. Your cross is one shot and it has to be believed. If he charges badly, you do not have to meet him, you can steer him past you. And the moment he over-commits, the legs are there.

    Why the roles are not called offense and defense

    The man applying pressure holds the fewest weapons here, and the man giving ground holds the most, including the takedown. Calling them offense and defense inverts what those words mean everywhere else and it reliably confuses the room. Closer and Spacer describe what each player is actually trying to do, which is the only thing either needs to remember.

    The Rules

    🤝 The Closer connects above the hipsDouble underhooks, any cinch around the body above the waist, or a cinch above the elbows that stops the arms being pulled free. Nothing below the hips: the lower half of the body is not the Closer's to take in this game. 🦵 The Spacer connects below the hipsThe level change is the Spacer's win, and it has to get under the hips onto the legs. The two players win in opposite halves of the same body, which is what keeps the exchange honest at both ends. 🧲 A clinch counts only if he cannot leaveThe proof is his failed escape, not your grip. If he slips an arm out, or pops you and separates, you did not have it. Arrive and then hold it through one honest attempt to go. 💣 One cross, then a full resetFrom rung 3 the Spacer holds a single rear cross, head or body. It can follow a jab or arrive alone, but once thrown, the hands reset completely before another. No machine-gunning jab into cross. Your cross is your bomb: miss with it and you have handed over the entry. ↩️ A redirect is a holdIf the Closer commits forward and the Spacer steers that momentum past themselves, the Spacer has kept their distance and won, even if the Closer ends up on the floor. Being put down by your own momentum is not a clinch. ✋ Hand controls are always liveWrist control, collar ties, framing and pummelling never switch off, at any rung, for either player. Adding strikes does not remove the hands. ⬛ Stay inside the perimeterBoth feet out loses the exchange, whoever you are. Without it the Spacer solves the game by walking backwards forever, which is not a solution to anything.

    How to Win

    Closer Clinch above the hips, held through one real escape attempt → switch roles.The observable proof: he tries to get out and fails. Hold roughly three seconds of honest resistance, or hold it through a visible attempt to slip the arm or shove off, whichever comes first. A grip he walks out of never happened.
    Spacer Connect below the hips on the legs → switch roles.A real level change that arrives under the hips with hands on the legs. A sprawl or a clean break means no count. Feinting the level change does not score.
    Spacer Redirect the committed entry and stay free → switch roles.He charged, you steered it past you, and you are still standing at range. Counts whether he stays up or goes down. This is the ruling to watch, see the note in Constraints-Led analysis.
    Spacer Break clean out of an attempted clinch → the exchange continues.Not a switch, a continuation. He got hands on you and you got out before it set, so the problem is live again from range. This is what stops the Closer from claiming every touch.
    Loss Both feet leave the perimeter.Instant loss regardless of position, for either player. It is the constraint that keeps the cage edge real.

    The Levels

    1Nothing in your handsThe Closer has no strikes at all.Closer: no punches, no kicks, hand controls and footwork only. Spacer: a jab, and the level change to the legs. This rung is entirely about patience and reading, because the Closer has no way to make anything happen except by arriving at the right moment.
    2The Closer earns a jabOne tool, used to open a door.The Closer gets a jab. It is not there to win exchanges, it is there to make the Spacer react so the entry has somewhere to go. Very Royce Gracie: the man coming in does not want a boxing match, he wants a grip.
    3The bombThe Spacer earns one resetting cross.A single rear cross, head or body, with a full reset after. It prices the walk-in, and it prices lazy jab-peppering. Its cost is built in: throw it and miss, and the retraction is the best entry the Closer will get all round.
    4The wall joins inThe environment goes live.Begin one body length off the wall. The Closer can now drag the Spacer onto it, and the Spacer has to keep their back off it. The clinch that could be walked out of in open space is much harder to leave once there is a wall behind you.
    5Finish itThe takedown is restored.The Closer may now convert the held clinch into a takedown or a wall pin rather than stopping at the connection. Only run this rung when the group has the position first, because a win condition reached from a bad position is not a win.

    Recall Check

    Test yourself before moving on. Answer out loud, then reveal what good looks like.

    Q You are the Closer and you have no weapons. Should you rush in and grab?
    No. There is no clock on the entry. Rushing is exactly what the Spacer's cross and level change are waiting for. Your job is to collect enough information that when you finally go, it works. Step in, see what comes back, adjust, and go once with high confidence rather than five times with low.
    Q You got both hands on him, then he shrugged you off. Did you win?
    No. The clinch is proven by his failure to leave, not by your grip arriving. If he slipped out or popped you off and made space, it did not count and the exchange is still live. The whole point of the position is that he cannot go.
    Q He charged you, you steered him past, and he fell over. Who won?
    You did, as the Spacer. Your job was to not be clinched and to still be free. He is on the floor because of his own momentum, and he never got hold of you. Note that this ruling is deliberately narrow: it is a Spacer win here, and we are not yet treating it as a general rule across every clinch-entry game.
    Q Why can the Closer only take the top half and the Spacer only the bottom half?
    Because it makes both jobs real at once. If the Closer could shoot, this would just be a takedown game. If the Spacer could clinch, they would stop having to keep distance. Splitting the body at the hips gives each player one honest route and forces them to actually solve the range problem between them.
    Q Should you apply more forward pressure?
    Only as much as buys you the reactions you want. More pressure is not more virtue. It raises your risk of being taken down or steered past, in direct proportion. Use enough to make him respond, use the jab to open the door, and keep the rest in your pocket.

    Go Deeper

    Task focus & coaching cues

    Each role's job

    🤝
    Closer

    Tight defence, calculated approach, one committed entry. Control with the free hand and punch with the other. Arrive above the hips and make him fail to leave.

    📏
    Spacer

    Volume on the jab so walking in costs. One believable cross. Read committed pressure and steer it. When his head travels ahead of his feet, take the legs.

    Coaching cues

    ⏳
    There is no time limit on the entry

    The most common Closer error is treating the round like a race. Say it plainly: you do not need to get there quickly, you need to get there. Gather data, keep a tight guard, and act when the confidence is high. A failed entry is worse than no entry, because the failure is what gets punished.

    🚫
    Stop reaching

    Students with no weapons reach out with both hands and get popped for it. Reaching announces the entry and gives away the arms. The hands stay home until the moment is bought and paid for.

    🔫
    The jab is a force field, not a scoring tool

    For the Spacer, volume is the job. Pump it so that anyone walking in eats two or three on the way. For the Closer, the jab exists to make him blink so the feet can travel, and nothing more.

    🎭
    Volume makes feints work

    Feints only land on someone who has been made to respect the real thing. Saturate the space with real shots first, then the feint, then the level. In that order, or the feint buys nothing.

    ✌️
    One hand controls, one hand punches

    Discovered live by a student rather than taught. The non-punching hand takes a wrist or a tie while the punching hand works, which occupies the Spacer's defence and buys the step. Worth letting the room find on their own before naming it.

    🎯
    Name the goal out loud, often

    This game confuses people more than most, because the man pressing forward is the one with nothing. Before each round have both players state their own win condition in one sentence. It costs ten seconds and prevents most of the stoppages.

    Constraints-Led analysis

    Constraints → Affordances

    Closer stripped of strikes→Entry has to be bought with timing and hand fighting rather than covered by punching
    Closer capped above the hips, Spacer capped below→Two live routes on one body, so neither player can collapse the game into a pure boxing or pure wrestling problem
    The clinch is proven by his failed escape→Rewards a grip that holds under load instead of a grip that merely arrives
    One cross with a full reset→Prices forward motion while building the punish window into the weapon itself, so overcommitment is self-correcting
    Redirect counts as keeping distance→Opens a second, non-collision answer to pressure and rewards reading the quality of the charge
    Perimeter→Removes infinite retreat, so distance has to be manufactured rather than taken

    The defining feature is the complementary win zone. The two players score in mutually exclusive halves of the same body, in the same exchange, which is unusual in the library and is what stops either from solving the game by changing what game it is. The second feature is the self-punishing weapon: the Spacer's cross does not need an external penalty because its own miss is the Closer's best entry, which is a cleaner design than a rule that says "do not overuse this".

    Open ruling, pending the mat: whether the redirect win generalises to the rest of the clinch-entry family, or stays specific to the version where the Closer is weapon-poor, is deliberately unresolved. Run it here, watch whether it degrades into flinching and pulling rather than genuine steering, and decide from that. Do not backport it until it has been tested.

    What athletes read

    👁️
    Visual

    Jab retraction and how lazily the hand comes home → the size of the entry window. Head travelling ahead of the feet → a charge that can be steered, or legs that can be taken.

    ✋
    Haptic

    Load arriving through a tie or a frame → whether he is set or reaching. Once connected, whether his weight is coming to you or leaving, which decides hold against re-clinch.

    🧭
    Proprioceptive

    Own hip-to-hip distance rather than glove distance, and where the perimeter sits behind the heels.

    What we measure (order parameter)

    The ratio of committed entries to successful ones. Early on students enter often and cheaply and get punished; the game has worked when entries become rarer, later, and far more likely to stick. Track entries attempted against clinches that survive an escape attempt, and watch whether the Closer's hands stay home between attempts.

    Representativeness

    Models: the grappler-versus-striker problem as it actually appears in a fight, where the man who wants the clinch has to cross the other man's best range to get it, and pays for every mistake with a takedown or a clean shot. The one-cross rule keeps the distance honest so the entry problem stays real.

    Closer above the hipsSpacer below the hipsthe clinch must survive an escape

    Inverts Pressure to Clinch, where the attacker escalates strikes and the defender denies. Here the man closing is the one disarmed. Sits opposite Clinch Denial, which is the pure-denial version of the Spacer's job, and feeds directly into 50/50 Clinch and Clinch Holder once the grip is made.

    First-run observations (2026-08-21)

    • Roles had to be re-explained four separate times in an hour under the offense and defense labels, which is why this page names them Closer and Spacer
    • Closers reached with both hands and were punished for it, consistently, at every rung
    • The patience framing ("no time clock, collect enough data") changed behaviour more than any rule adjustment
    • A student independently discovered the control-hand and punch-hand split, which is the game producing the solution rather than the coach
    • Run in an odd-numbered rotation, which added to the role confusion; pair the group evenly if you can
    • Run without a marked perimeter live, the perimeter is our addition for consistency with the rest of the library

    Readiness to progress

    • Enters once, well, rather than repeatedly and cheaply
    • Keeps the hands home until the entry is bought
    • Holds the clinch through an honest escape attempt rather than claiming the touch
    • As Spacer, throws the cross like it is real and does not spend it carelessly
    • Reads a bad charge and steers it instead of meeting it head on

    Warning signs

    Reaches with both hands from outside range Treats the round as a race and charges repeatedly Counts a grip the partner walked straight out of Drifts to a range nobody would really stand at because the other man cannot punch Throws the cross soft, so it prices nothing Announces a read to the partner instead of using it

    Safety & related games

    🧠 Head up when you drive, especially from rung 4. Dropping the head while your partner leaves the wall is how necks get spiked. If you are going to disappear, disappear into the wall, not into the floor 🤛 Light gloves, controlled jab and cross. The cross has to be believable, not damaging 🖐️ Mind the fingers in the hand fighting, no grips into the glove or the face ⚖️ Size mismatches: run rungs 1 to 4 and hold the takedown at rung 5 until both players are comfortable
