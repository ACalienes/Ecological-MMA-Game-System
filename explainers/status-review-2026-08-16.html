Both arrived here
A game is one canonical page. Variations live on that page, named, with the change stated plainly.
Your 62 games probably do not want to become 200. They likely want to become roughly 40 canonical games carrying 160 named variations. A smaller library that reads as a deeper one.
Think of it the way a cookbook does. There is one chili recipe. Underneath it: "make it vegetarian", "make it for a crowd", "make it hotter". Nobody argues about whether those are four recipes or one. The argument disappears because the relationship is written down instead of remembered.

Your fear, diagnosed

The system guesses instead of asking. That is the whole problem.

Every reviewer found a version of the same thing, and it is the root of your classification worry too. Almost nothing in the library ever states plainly what it is. The software infers it from soft signals, and the guesses are sometimes wrong.

Your worry about endless arguing is the human-facing version of a system that never asks anyone to write anything down. Which is why the fix works: declare it, do not infer it.

The three things that are actually broken

Found by Codex, verified by me against the real data before I repeated any of it. Ordered by how much they hurt a paying customer.

1
Plans tell people to run a game while hiding its foundation
What happens: Get Your Game Plan builds an ordered plan, then quietly drops games that did not fit the filter, even when a game it kept depends on one it dropped. So the plan says "run Ground Access" without ever mentioning the two games Ground Access is built on.
Why it matters: the order of the games is the expertise you are selling. This is the one that makes the product look like it does not know what it is doing.
the scale of it
42 of your 62 games have a prerequisite that is intermediate or harder. The rescue pass in the code only pulls back beginner-level prerequisites, so most of the library can hit this.
2
One finished game is invisible, right now, on the live site
What happens: Positional Sparring is finished and published. It carries the tag "format". The software reads that word and removes the game from every single recommendation, and skips almost all quality checks on it.
Why it matters: a word in a list is silently deciding what customers are allowed to be shown. Any game tagged that way vanishes the same way.
where it lives
build-game-index.py line 189 sets is_format from the tag text. your-plan.js line 163 excludes those from all plans. eco-check.py line 66 skips quality checks on them.
3
Nothing in the library ever says it is finished
What happens: not one of your 62 games declares itself finished. 39 have no status at all, and the software treats "no status" as "finished, go ahead and recommend this to paying customers."
Why it matters: being ready for customers is decided by a field being absent. That is a coin flip dressed as a rule.

What Kai wants you to hear

Do not sell the customizer. Sell the curriculum.

The classification problem is your pain as the author. It hurts every time you add content. Handing customers a game builder makes your pain go away by moving the work onto them, and calling it a feature.

A coach with 45 minutes, twelve students and a 7pm start does not want a generator. They want to know what to run tonight. They are paying for the removal of a decision, and a customizer sells the decision back to them with extra steps.

So: the catalog is the product, the generator is the engine. Build the engine. Do not put it on the box.

Authority is not knowing everything. It is knowing what must not change.

You worried that letting people customize gives away the reason they paid. Kai's answer flips it: an open-ended form reads as "we do not know either, you figure it out." But saying "these three things you may change, these two are load-bearing and here is why" reads as more expert than a fixed page, not less.

One warning worth taking seriously. Kai flagged the idea of letting customers record their own classes and get games back. Not because it is hard, but because it means audio of other people's gyms, possibly including minors, being processed into a product. You already handle this correctly, since class audio never leaves your machine. Extending it to other people's gyms is a different question, and the safer version is a private report on what a coach actually drilled, not a public product.
