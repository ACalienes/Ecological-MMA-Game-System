## Headline

The hour is a single thread: **closing the distance against a striker, then holding what you closed.** Three games, run in sequence, each one starting where the last one ended. Game 1 (pre-recording) is the clinch entry with no weapons; Game 2 arms both sides asymmetrically; Game 3 starts already clinched and asks whether you can keep it.

Three things make this class unusually valuable as product input.

**One: the class contains a complementary-zone constraint that the library has never encoded.** The clinch-seeker may only connect **above the hips** (11:47, 15:36). The distance-keeper wins by connecting **below the hips, on the legs** (15:02, 20:46). The two players win in mutually exclusive vertical zones on the same body, in the same exchange. That is a genuinely elegant piece of game design and it is nowhere in the 62 pages. It also resolves cleanly against the existing above-the-knee connection law, because it constrains a different player for a different reason.

**Two: this is the inverse of `pressure-to-clinch`, not a level of it.** In the library, the clinch attacker escalates strikes level by level and the defender denies. Here the clinch-seeker is **weapon-poor by design** and the distance-keeper is **weapon-rich**, including the takedown. The pressure player has to earn the clinch while paying for every step forward. Per the sibling rule (same structure, different goals = different games), this is a sibling page, not a Level 6.

**Three: the role labels actively broke the class.** Alex had to stop and re-explain who was who at 14:07, 17:45, 22:08, and 53:58, and conceded the definition himself at 28:30: *"Offense just means forward pressure person. That's really all that is."* Four full stoppages in a 61-minute class is a design defect in the vocabulary, not in the students. See The Naming Problem, this is the most immediately actionable finding in the report.

## The progression

### Game 1 (pre-recording, re-stated at 11:35 - 12:29): the unarmed clinch entry

Alex re-explains it on tape specifically because the first run was not recorded: "we're going to escalate the game now, just because I didn't record it the last time" (11:35). Useful: it means we have his clean, considered statement of the rules rather than a live scramble.

| Element | Rule |
|---|---|
| Closer's weapons | **None.** No punches, no kicks. |
| Closer's win | Establish a close-range clinch: double underhooks, any cinch **above the waist**, or a cinch **above the elbows**, enough that the partner cannot pull their arms out or create separation. |
| Spacer's weapons | Jab only. |
| Spacer's win | Maintain distance, **or** level change and connect on the legs, taking advantage of forward momentum and clumsiness. |
| Rotation | Winner switches roles (00:35). |

The stated intent (12:17): the jab is there to keep distance *and* to hold a standing threat, "that if somebody is Frankenstein walking at you, you can level change and connect on them."

### Game 2 (12:31 - 33:50): arm both sides, asymmetrically

One addition to each player.

- **Closer** gets a jab. Used to open windows, not to box. "Very Royce Gracie style" (14:55).
- **Spacer** gets a jab plus **one cross**, and the cross is rate-limited: *"the cross is only one at a time. If you decide to throw a cross, you throw it, you have to completely reset. So we're not able to machine gun between jab and cross"* (12:47). No pre-requisite jab needed; a naked cross is legal but spends the same budget (13:09).
- **"Your cross is your bomb. So use it wisely. If you throw it and you miss or you fuck up, chances are that's the best time for somebody to come in and take advantage and close that distance"** (13:16).

That last line is the whole design: the cross is not just capped, it has a **built-in punish window**. Overcommit and you have handed the closer the entry. This is a materially better constraint than a flat "one strike per exchange" cap because the cost is positional and immediate rather than bookkept.

### Game 3 (33:52 - 55:20): hold what you closed

The class's second half, and where the reps piled up, Alex extended it twice by student request (23:38, 45:06).

| Element | Rule |
|---|---|
| Start | Already in the **50/50 clinch**. |
| Holder's goal | Maintain the clinch, do not get taken down, **and land as much damage as possible while keeping it**. |
| Escaper's goal | Separate as fast as possible, **or** get under the hips and connect on the leg. |
| Strikes | Simulated. Knees are thrown with the **thigh to the side of the body**, not the actual knee (35:57). Dirty boxing live. Plum, collar ties, any clinch morph legal. |
| Environment | **Live.** "By the way, the environment is in play" (37:45). The wall is repeatedly named as the holder's asset: "It's your friend, not his" (38:31), "that wall is going to be your best friend" (39:50). |
| Explicitly excluded | The finishing takedown. "What I would have said is that we would have gone to a takedown, but I don't want that today" (34:44), size disparity in the room. |
| Gold star | Get to the back and stay there. "That's his fucking problem to deal with" (43:48). |

**The reason the takedown was cut is itself a coaching point** (34:54): he does not want students rewarded for reaching a winning *condition* from a bad *position*. "I don't want us being complacent that this is good... the winning condition is a metaphor for the idea that we're gonna be in a place to take somebody down." The win condition is a proxy, and a proxy reached badly is not a win. That is a sharper statement of the threshold philosophy than anything currently written down.

## Rulings worth capturing

**1. The stickiness proof standard (24:00).** The clinch only counts if the partner cannot leave. *"When I go in to clinch somebody, I don't want them to leave. If Leo tries to pull away and I can keep him, then I know that I did it. If I go to clinch him and he's able to slip his arm out or he bops me and is able to separate, then I didn't do it."* This is the threshold house rule applied to a connection rather than a position, the proof is the partner's failed escape, not the grip itself.

**2. Redirect counts as a hold (24:33).** Alex flags this as something he hoped a student would discover and then illuminates it himself: if the closer commits forward momentum and the spacer **redirects them past or down**, the spacer has satisfied the requirement, even though the closer hit the ground. *"By them falling to the ground they've not clinched me, I still have satisfied my requirements."* Note this is a win for the spacer via the closer's own momentum, distinct from the level-change takedown win.

**3. Forward pressure is priced, not free (25:29).** A student asks directly whether he should apply more forward pressure. *"More forward pressure lends itself to higher risk of being taken down or being redirected... use enough forward pressure that gives you reactions that you want."* Pressure is a dial with a cost curve, not a virtue.

**4. There is no clock on the entry (11:18).** *"There's no inherent time clock that I need to get there right away. I need to collect enough data to feel good about it. That's the real answer."* Paired with 09:58: *"I need to have high confidence that when I act, it'll work."* Explicit patience-as-skill, and it maps directly onto the existing `confidence-rating` concept, this is field evidence for that page.

**5. Losing the game and playing it well are different (06:32).** After a student is clinched: *"You lost the game, but the reaction was good... He got to the clinch, so technically he won the game, but the way that you reacted, everything was good. There's nothing bad about it."* Repeated at 20:55: *"Your reactions, it's not bad. It's just the rules of this game."* Worth surfacing in product copy, it is the answer to "did I do it wrong."

**6. Neck safety under wall pressure (44:20).** Attached to Nate's spike. *"You're here like this and your head's down, he's removing himself from the wall and you're falling into it... anything that we're doing, we want our head to have this kind of upward position so that we're not spiking ourselves on the top of our head. If I'm going to disappear, I'm going to disappear into the wall."* This belongs on the wall pages as a safety note; it is the first neck-spike incident in the transcript history.

## New game candidates

### A. Closing the Distance (working title): the Game 2 shape

The strongest candidate in the class, and the one with the most tape behind it.

- Closer: jab only, wins by a sticky clinch **above the hips**.
- Spacer: jab plus **one resetting cross**, wins by maintaining distance, by redirecting committed forward momentum, or by level-changing to connect **below the hips** on the legs.
- Loss conditions and perimeter per house standard.

Sibling to `pressure-to-clinch`, not a level of it, because the goals invert: there, the attacker escalates strikes; here, the closer is deliberately disarmed and the *spacer* holds the heavier arsenal plus the takedown. Cross-link both ways, plus `clinch-denial` (the spacer's pure-denial cousin) and `counter-wrestling`.

Levels write themselves from the class: **L1** closer unarmed (Game 1) → **L2** closer gets the jab → **L3** spacer gets the one-cross bomb → **L4** wall live → **L5** takedown finish restored.

### B. Clinch Holder vs. Escaper: the Game 3 shape

Asymmetric counterpart to the existing `fifty-fifty-clinch`, which is symmetric ("either fighter" wins four neutral ways). Here one player holds and damages, the other escapes or shoots. Same start, opposite goals, textbook sibling.

Notable that the holder must **damage while holding**, not merely hold. That is the anti-stall mechanism built into the win condition itself rather than bolted on as a tax, which is cleaner than the `fifty-fifty-clinch` L3 approach.

Check for overlap with `wall-grinding` and `wall-control` before building, the wall half of this may already be covered, in which case the open-space start is the novel part.

## Tensions with existing pages

**1. `pressure-to-clinch` role semantics.** The page frames the clinch-seeker as "attacker" with escalating strike access. The class runs the opposite and calls the same player "offense" while giving them nothing. Anyone reading the page and then attending class gets contradictory models. At minimum the new sibling page needs to say explicitly how it differs.

**2. `fifty-fifty-clinch` is `status: wip`** and its win conditions are neutral/symmetric. The class gives us a fully specified asymmetric variant with 21 minutes of live reps behind it. Good moment to either bless the existing page and add the sibling, or reconsider the page in light of this.

**3. The above-the-hips constraint is unwritten.** `one-shot-discovery` encodes "connection on legs under the waist, above the knee (no body lock, no grabs below the knee)" for the *counter*. The class adds the complementary half, the clinch-seeker's ceiling. The glossary should carry both halves together, since they are one rule seen from two sides.

**4. Muay Thai clinch framing (32:54 - 33:25).** *"Muay Thai clinching in MMA is completely useless, in my opinion. That's why you don't see MMA fighters doing it... they have no threat of the level change. So the game is totally different."* This is a strong, quotable rationale for why the 50/50 clinch pages exist at all, and it currently appears nowhere. It is opinion, flagged as such by Alex, treat as voice, not doctrine.

## Concept candidates

**1. Three Points of Pressure (55:46 - 59:13).** The most technically specific block of the class, delivered as a closing lecture with Nate as uke. Forehead into the head/spine, shoulder/center-mass on the top side, hip driving low. *"Between these three things I've got these three points of contact that are really nice to make it hard for him to escape."* Attributed to Dagestani and Russian wrestlers, plus a Diaz-brothers application: pin, then transfer forehead-to-chest and work from there; when he stops running, go back to striking. Forehead pressure was coached live four separate times (41:31, 52:15, 52:22, 55:46) before the lecture, so it is already a live cue.

**2. Strikes as Reaction Tools (57:40 - 58:35).** Both roles, stated back to back. On offense: *"Everything that we're doing from a striking perspective is to establish... we're getting closer to our goal."* On defense: *"My goal here is not to fucking knock him out with these punches. What I want to do is get him to react, to get my opening."* This is the general principle behind 8/14's disincentive weapon, and the two classes together make a strong case for a standalone concept page. Also ties to the volume-feint relationship at 31:48: *"the more volume you throw, then the more the feints are gonna work, then the levels are gonna work. You're saturating the field."*

**3. Check, Not Checkmate (46:26).** The hip-to-hip side position with one hand free and the opponent's weapons pointed away. *"This is kind of like the check. It's not checkmate, but it's check. The guy's got nothing, all of his weapons are facing that way."* A named intermediate-dominance state, distinct from a finish. Possible fit as a section on an existing page rather than its own concept.

**4. Student-discovered affordance, credited (26:14).** Ray independently found that the non-punching hand controls while the punching hand punches, and Alex stopped the room to name it: *"Ray's actually doing a really good job of that... he's discovered that if I use my non-punching hand to control and my punching hand to punch, this is a pretty good deal."* Worth logging as field evidence that the constraint design produced the intended discovery, which is exactly what the eco-psych framing predicts.

## The Naming Problem

The single most actionable finding.

Roles were misunderstood or reversed at **14:07, 17:45, 22:08, and 53:58**, each requiring a full stop. Sampled confusion:

- 14:14: *"Oh, I thought you had a takedown." / "No, no, no. So let's redefine."*
- 22:08: *"You were defense that last one." / "I was offense." / "No, defense." / "Defense goes for the clinch?" / "Defense goes for the legs."*
- 53:58: *"But who's offense there? Who's maintaining the clinch?" / "I thought it was you."*

The cause is structural: the player called **offense** has *no weapons*, and the player called **defense** holds the *jab, the cross, and the takedown*. The labels invert the students' priors from every other game in the system. Alex effectively conceded this at 28:30 and again at 53:11 (*"One person's trying to stay sticky, one person's trying to get out"*), both times he reverted to **descriptive** labels and the confusion stopped.

**Recommendation:** on the new pages, drop offense/defense entirely for this family and use role-descriptive names: **Closer / Spacer** for Game 2, **Holder / Escaper** for Game 3. Alex's own fallback phrasings ("forward pressure person", "sticky", "trying to get out") are the source material. This is cheap, testable in one class, and it directly addresses four stoppages.

Secondary contributor: the odd-numbered rotation (22:49, *"I think it's the three man that's kind of..."*). Worth watching whether the confusion tracks group parity rather than vocabulary, but the transcript points at vocabulary.
