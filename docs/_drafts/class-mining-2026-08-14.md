## Headline

The whole hour is a single thread: **the leg kick**, run as one game re-layered five times, then inverted a sixth. "We're going to play a thread with the leg kick all day today" (22:04). This is the most disciplined single-thread progression in the transcript history, and the layering is the point, not the individual games.

Two things make this class unusually valuable as product input.

**One: it contains a clean, unplanned demonstration of turning a coaching cue into a constraint.** At 22:29 Alex verbally corrects a range problem: students were standing far too close because the defender had no punches, so closing distance carried no cost. That is a representativeness defect introduced by the weapon-removal constraint itself, and the first patch was an instruction ("respect the MMA landscape"). Two minutes later, at 24:53, he patches it structurally instead by giving the defender a single rear straight that **cannot win the game** and exists only to make closing expensive. Instruction became constraint inside the same session. That is a first-class eco-psych move and it is worth a concept page on its own.

**Two: the class contradicts what two current library pages say to do.** Alex corrected the reset-after-a-win habit three separate times (21:05, 35:47, and again through the sparring cues), ruling that a win which does not switch roles must not pause. `rear-leg-offense.md` and `lead-leg-offense.md` currently encode the opposite ("Land clean → switch", "Whiffed or blocked → reset", "Defender has no win condition (diagnostic only)"). Secondary: `winning-the-circle` and `finishing-sequence` now show up as live coaching vocabulary during sparring (55:44, 87:57, 88:02, 88:07). The concept pages have made it into the way the room is coached, which is the first direct evidence of that.

## The progression (00:17 - 71:07)

The base game never changes shape. Offense has a lead hand and a rear leg. Defense is trying to not get leg-kicked. Every layer adds one thing.

**Shared rules across Games 1-5**

| Element | Rule |
|---|---|
| Offense weapons | Lead hand only, rear leg only. No rear hand, no lead leg. |
| Target | Above the knee, below the hip. Inside or outside, open or closed stance, all legal. |
| Contact | Lower shin. Toes or instep does not count, and the kicker is expected to call it on themselves. |
| Stance | Switching is allowed, but the weapon constraints follow the new stance ("if you switch your stance, you have to respect that", 20:44). |
| Calf kicks | Banned at 03:36, then opened to experimentation at 07:07. |
| Rotation | Winner stays in, groups cycle. |

### Game 1 (00:17 - 14:40): land the leg kick vs. check the leg kick

Offense uses the lead hand to break tempo and build patterns, then lands the rear leg kick. Defense has **no strikes at all**, but may use hands and guard for hand controls, plus footwork. Defense wins by checking. Offense wins by landing clean, and **continues on offense** rather than switching.

**The deliberate proof asymmetry (01:57):** "the definition for checking a kick is more vague than it is to land. You need to land accurately... if I pick up my leg and it didn't feel perfect, but it didn't land, I'm going to count that as a win. So it's on the offensive person to make sure they're landing cleanly." The harder-to-judge action gets the benefit of the doubt; the cleaner-to-judge action carries the burden of proof. This is new. It is not the same as the threshold house rule, which is about observable proof; this is about **which side owns the proof**.

### Intervention A (11:58 - 14:40): play the game, not generic MMA

The universal note of the class, and the one Alex spent the most time on. Students were moving too much on defense, which is generic MMA behavior, not a solution to this game.

- "How do I take the rules that we've established and set myself up strategically to win as easily as possible... a lot of times we get into this rhythm, we know the game, we understand it, but we're still kind of playing like a general MMA game."
- The specific logic: if your only win condition is the check, then movement costs you the perch you need, **and you actually want him to kick you**, because his kick is the only thing that can produce your win. "By moving away, I'm reducing that opportunity."
- Model given: the hyper-defensive Muay Thai posture, spring-loaded to check and counter, because in this game there is no penalty for not leaving.

A student names the cause out loud at 07:56: "we've been playing a lot of footwork games." **This is transfer interference between our own games**, not a random error. Worth taking seriously as a curriculum-sequencing signal.

### Game 2 (14:42 - 22:00): defense can also win by returning fire

Same game, one addition. Defense now wins by checking **or** by eating the kick and returning a leg kick. "Last person who kicks wins" (33:49).

### Intervention B (22:17 - 24:53): three notes

1. **Range integrity.** "Even though we're playing a specific game, you should respect the MMA landscape. I see a lot of guys fighting super close, and because this guy can't punch me, I'm getting comfortable with the idea of being this close. I'm never going to be that close." (See Headline: this becomes a constraint two minutes later.)
2. **Feint richness.** Feint with the leg, feint by dropping the stance, feint the level, feint repeatedly. Cites a study that UFC fighters feint seven to ten times before committing to a strike. Also handles the earlier student question well: you may feint the rear hand, but a feint with a weapon your opponent knows you do not have is not credible.
3. **The lead hand is a weight tool, not a damage tool.** "I can use this lead hand to manipulate his weight. If I throw a lead hook and put weight into his body, he's going to put weight down on that lead leg. I jab, I hook, I kick." Also the counter to the hyper-defensive leg-up posture ("he's karate kid and he just keeps his leg up"): use the lead hand to break the balance and take the timing.

### Game 3 (24:53 - 35:45): the disincentive weapon

Defense gets **one weapon: a single rear straight**, head or body. No hooks, no uppercuts, no overhands. Critically, **it cannot win the game** (25:53: "You cannot win by landing the cross"). Its entire function is to make closing distance and lazy jab-peppering expensive. Gold star introduced here: check **and** return a kick.

### Intervention C (35:47 - 36:35): no reset on a non-switching win

"If I'm playing offensively and my goal is to land the kick, and if I kick successfully I keep going, it makes no sense for me to do this: kick, I did it, oh good job, all right, now I'm going to start again... If I get in this habit where every time I do something good I take a step back and relax, it's a bad habit to put into play. If you're winning on a continuous contingency, continue."

Flagged live at 21:05, 34:15, 35:15, and again at 44:xx. It kept recurring, which suggests the habit is coming from somewhere structural, and see the library contradiction below.

### Game 4 (36:35 - 47:24): the check stops being a win

The escalation. **Checking alone no longer wins.** You must check *and* kick back, or eat the kick and return one. Whoever lands the last kick wins, which makes it an **infinite exchange ladder**: "this could go all day long, in theory."

Intent stated plainly: "as soon as I feel pressure, I'm coming back with one. And if something happens, I could be blind, I'm going to fire that kick because I don't want to lose."

Safety framing attached to this layer specifically: "this game probably has a tendency to up intensity, so let's keep it academic and let's not let it escalate from the standpoint of ego. If you get got, that's okay. Be fast. It doesn't mean you go slow or be timid."

Continuation clarification (39:38): you continue only if you are on offense and landed the last kick. Lose, and you pause, swap, restart.

### Intervention D (47:28 - 51:20): three notes

1. **Safety, and the biggest single-topic block of the class.** Two students hurt the top of the foot. "You'll hurt the top of your foot if you kick it on someone's knee or elbow. It will happen, I guarantee it, I've broken my feet. This is probably the shittiest part of the MMA game." Ruling: landing on the instep, even painlessly, is a **red flag on your distancing**, not a scoring rep. Shin only.
2. **Pattern data is being noticed but not used.** "If every time we're throwing the cross we're getting kicked for it, that's useful data... what if I throw that cross knowing he's going to come and check?" And the sharp version: "Don't tell him. Do something about it." Students were verbalizing reads to their partner instead of exploiting them.
3. **A disincentive that is not respected does not exist.** Students threw the cross soft and slow, "just because that's a tool that I happen to have," so it did not function. "If someone isn't believing your disincentivizing shots, you need to step it up... This is the difference between calibrating power and going into ego mode. You should have a read that says, I threw that cross and he really didn't do anything about it, I have to dial it a little bit more so he takes it seriously."

### Game 5 (51:20 - 61:00): the shot goes live

Offense keeps everything and adds a win: **connect on the leg** (level change to a real connection). A sprawl or a break means no count. The reciprocal constraint is the interesting half: **showing the level change is an invitation for defense to counter with a kick**. "You don't just get to feint it all day long. If you feinted, it's an invitation to counter."

Failed shot where defense breaks → switch. Successful shot → continue (57:15).

Coaching attached (53:14): moment-by-moment reads and the posture trade-off. Upright and eyeing the legs means a kick is coming; head dropping and level threats mean you need to shift from check-ready to sprawl-ready. "This is the chess game."

### Intervention E (60:37 - 61:52): cycle the weapons to break the pattern

"If I'm always jabbing and kicking and I never show the wrestling, then when I show the wrestling it's novel for him... just because I took him down before, it's going to leave an impression. He did it, but he could do it again." The threat persists after the action. "They're simply thinking that you're going to throw a rock and you're throwing paper."

### Game 6 (61:52 - 71:07): the inversion

Correctly identified by a student as "the polar opposite of what we were doing."

| | Offense | Defense |
|---|---|---|
| Weapons | Hands only: one jab **or** one cross, head or body. No hooks, no uppercuts. Feints allowed. | Leg kicks only. **Either leg**, lead or rear. Shin only. No hand strikes, but hands are live for grips and controls. |
| Wins by | Landing the jab or the cross. Also wins by **checking** the incoming kick, which lets them continue. | Landing any leg kick, which takes them to offense. |

Deliberately framed as "a high repetition game, I want it high activity." Note this is the only game of the six where the **lead leg** is legal, and the only one where checking is an offensive player's tool.

### Debrief (71:09 - 73:21): where your feet end up

Two students set up kicks by stepping off to the side and switching stance, which jams them and leaves them unable to return fire. "Two cats in a cloud of dust." Cue: think through where the positioning of your feet will be **when the kick is finished**. "If I know that my game is kick and don't be kicked, or have a high frequency of return fire, then I need to make sure that I'm not putting myself in a place where I can't do that." Angles are still fine, over-committing to a weird kick is not.

**Granby roll (78:19 - 81:35),** taught to one student for escaping chest-to-back. Cues: step to the outside, get **perpendicular** ("that's the cue that you're in the right spot"), then roll inside shoulder over the far shoulder, belly facing away. ## Divergences (intent vs. reception vs. adjustment)

1. **Check the kick / students moved constantly / stay and be spring-loaded.** Cause named by a student: carryover from the footwork games. Real transfer interference between our own curriculum items. The game brief never told them what winning cheaply looks like.
2. **Continuous offense / students reset after every success / explicit no-reset ruling, three times.** See the library contradiction below. This one is probably our fault, not theirs.
3. **Rear straight as a disincentive / thrown soft, so ignored / dial it up, and the calibration-vs-ego distinction.** A disincentive weapon needs a believability threshold or it is not a constraint at all.
4. **Isolate the leg kick / students fought at an unrealistically close range / verbal patch, then a structural patch.** The constraint distorted the affordance landscape. The fix was to add a weapon back.
5. **Adapt from what you observe / students verbalized reads instead of exploiting them / "don't tell him, do something about it."**
6. **Shin contact / two students kicked knees with the instep and got hurt / instep contact reframed as a distancing red flag rather than a bad rep.** Safety, and it landed twice in one hour.

## How this maps to the library

**Direct contradiction to fix (highest confidence item in this report).** `rear-leg-offense.md` and `lead-leg-offense.md` both read "Land clean → switch · Whiffed or blocked → reset · Defender has no win condition (diagnostic only)." Today's Game 1 is the same start position with the opposite finish logic: land clean → **continue**, and the defender has a real win condition. Alex coached against the reset habit four times in one hour. At minimum the reset language needs to change; more likely these two pages want today's live version as the progression that follows the diagnostic.

**`kick-defense.md` (Kick Defense Isolation, WIP, born 2026-06-20)** is the closest relative but is not the same game. It covers all roundhouses with check/block/catch and a marked perimeter. Today is leg kicks only, no catch at all, offense weapon-restricted to lead hand plus rear leg, and the win conditions **escalate across the layers** rather than sitting as optional levels. The escalation is what makes it work, and nothing in the library currently expresses "the previous win condition stops counting."

**`win-ladder.md`** is tagged as a format but lives in ground/wrestling. Game 4 is an infinite last-action-wins ladder in striking. Possibly the same format applied to a new domain, possibly a distinct mechanic, since Win Ladder is a handicap/scaling device and this is a continuation rule.

**`counter-striking.md`** is adjacent to Game 6 but not the same: today both sides are weapon-restricted, and in Game 3 the counter is a weapon that explicitly cannot win.

**`lead-hand-offense.md`** should pick up the weight-manipulation cue (jab, hook to load his lead leg, then kick), which is a different job for the lead hand than the page currently gives it.

**Concepts already landing:** `winning-the-circle` and `finishing-sequence` are being coached by name in sparring.

**New house rule and glossary candidates,** in rough order of how load-bearing they are:

- **No reset on a non-switching win.** If your win does not change roles, do not pause. Applies library-wide. `sustained-offense.md` already encodes the same principle for a single game ("single shots that reset don't count"), which suggests it should be promoted.
- **Asymmetric proof burden.** The action that is harder to judge gets the benefit of the doubt; the action that is cleaner to judge carries the burden. Sits next to the threshold house rule but answers a different question.
- **A disincentive must be respected to exist.** With the calibration-versus-ego distinction as the safety half.
- **Range integrity under weapon removal.** When a constraint removes a threat, the range it was policing goes unrealistic unless something replaces it. Preferred fix is a constraint, not a reminder.
- **Play to win the game, not to fight.** If a student is playing generic MMA inside a constrained game, the game is not doing its work yet.
- **Exploit the pattern, do not announce it.**
- **Shin, not instep.** Safety block for every kicking game. Instep contact is a distancing error, not a landed kick.

## Answered by Alex, 2026-08-16

**Q1, Q2 (structure and home): ANSWERED.** Games 1-5 are one game with the escalation ladder, Game 6 is a separate sibling, and both are leg-kick-specific games rather than levels folded into `kick-defense.md`. Built and shipped as `check-and-return.md` and `kick-the-puncher.md`.

**Q3, Q4 (the reset contradiction, and promoting no-reset to a house rule): ANSWERED, and the report was wrong to frame it as a contradiction.** Alex's ruling: *"Some games are gonna have student resets, and games are gonna require continuation, so we can classify them, but neither one is wrong. It just depends contextually on what the focus of the game is."*

So `rear-leg-offense.md` and `lead-leg-offense.md` are **not** broken. They are reset-type games, which is a legitimate design, and no-reset must **not** become a universal house rule. What this actually calls for is a declared field, `exchange: reset` or `exchange: continuous`, so the choice is stated on each page rather than argued about. That is the same declare-do-not-infer principle as the `type` and `status` work, and it belongs with the canonical/variation field rather than as a rule change. **No edits were made to those two pages.**

**A related ruling that changes how difficulty works across the whole library:** *"An intermediate game essentially has more variables than what a beginner game would have. Any game can be simplified down to make it easier for somebody to focus on fewer things... Games can go from being as binary all the way up to the infinitely nuanced live experience that is a live sparring session or a real fight."*

Difficulty is therefore **a dial, not a fixed property**. This resolved the recommender's beginner problem directly: when a goal has no beginner content, the plan now still offers the easiest tier and explicitly tells the athlete to strip it back (fewer weapons, one win condition, slower pace, add a variable back each round) instead of either dead-ending them or silently pretending an intermediate game is a beginner one. It also reframes the existing per-page Levels steppers as points on that dial rather than separate difficulty tiers.

**Q10 (WIP games at launch): ANSWERED.** Not hide, not present with a caveat. *"We just need to finish the unfinished games."* The 25 WIP pages are a work item, not a presentation decision.
